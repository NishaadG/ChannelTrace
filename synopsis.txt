CHANNELTRACE
Multi-Channel Digital Marketing Attribution and Conversion Analysis
Synopsis

1. Abstract
Customers rarely buy after seeing a single ad. A typical buyer may arrive through a social media post, later return through a Google search, come back directly and only then make a purchase. This project studies such multi-channel customer journeys using public web analytics and advertising data, compares how different attribution models divide the credit for a conversion among channels, and identifies which visitor and session behaviours are associated with a purchase. The research methods are then packaged into ChannelTrace, a free web application with three parts: a dashboard presenting our research findings, a plug-and-play module where a business uploads its own Google Ads, Meta (Facebook) Ads or customer-journey CSV and receives an insight dashboard, and an AI assistant that explains the results in plain language and suggests actions. The tool and the assistant are themselves evaluated for correctness and usability. The entire project is built with free and open-source tools at zero cost.

2. Problem Statement
Businesses can see that a sale happened but usually cannot tell which marketing interactions led to it. Each ad platform reports its own view, typically last-click, so several platforms may claim credit for the same sale. We identify three gaps. First, a measurement gap: the credit given to a channel changes considerably depending on the attribution rule used, and most small teams never compare rules. Second, a tooling gap: attribution tools are either tied to one platform or require paid subscriptions. Third, an interpretation gap: even when numbers are available, small business owners often do not know what action to take from them.

3. Aim
To study how multiple digital marketing touchpoints relate to customer conversion, to show how different attribution models change the credit assigned to each channel, and to deliver and evaluate these methods as a free, easy-to-use tool that businesses can apply to their own data.

4. Objectives
a) Collect, clean and document four complementary public datasets.
b) Reconstruct multi-session customer journeys and identify common channel paths to purchase.
c) Compare seven attribution models: first touch, last touch, linear, time decay, position based, and two data-driven models (Markov chain removal effect and Shapley value).
d) Identify session and visitor behaviours associated with conversion using statistical tests and machine learning.
e) Build an interactive dashboard that presents the research findings.
f) Build a plug-and-play module that accepts CSV exports from Google Ads, Meta Ads or journey logs and generates an insight dashboard automatically.
g) Build an AI assistant that answers questions and suggests actions, grounded only in the computed results.
h) Evaluate the tool for correctness, usability and assistant accuracy.
i) Write a research paper covering methodology, results, evaluation, limitations and practical implications.

5. Research Questions
RQ1. What are the common channel journeys before a customer converts?
RQ2. How do converting and non-converting sessions and journeys differ?
RQ3. Does the estimated importance of a channel change when a different attribution model is used?
RQ4. Which customer or session characteristics are associated with a higher likelihood of conversion?
RQ5. Can these methods be packaged into a tool that gives small businesses correct and usable plain-language insights from their own marketing data?

6. Proposed Solution
The project has three connected components.
Research study: journey, attribution and conversion analysis on public datasets, reported in a research paper with original results.
ChannelTrace dashboard: a web application with a Research Findings section and a Your Data section. In Your Data, a business uploads a CSV; the tool detects the file type and column names, cleans the data and generates the dashboard with no login, API setup or cost.
AI marketing assistant: a chatbot that reads the dashboard's computed metrics (never the raw uploaded data) and answers questions such as "Which campaign should I cut?" or "Which channel is undervalued by last-click?" in plain language.

This differs from a conventional reporting dashboard in three ways: it is driven by research questions, it works on any business's own data rather than one fixed dataset, and it compares several attribution models instead of showing a single number.

7. Data
Each dataset is matched to the research question it can genuinely answer. The datasets come from different populations and will not be merged.

a) Google Analytics 360 sample, Google Merchandise Store (BigQuery public dataset google_analytics_sample): 12 months (August 2016 to August 2017) of session-level web analytics with a persistent visitor ID, visit number, visit start time, the default channel grouping (Organic Search, Paid Search, Social, Referral, Display, Affiliates, Direct), traffic source and medium, and transactions with revenue. It is the only dataset with several named marketing channels per customer journey, so it is the primary source for RQ1 and RQ3. Visitor IDs are obfuscated and some fields are removed by Google.

b) UCI Online Shoppers Purchasing Intention (Sakar and Kastro, 2018): 12,330 website sessions from a one-year period, with 17 behavioural features and a purchase outcome. Used for RQ2 and RQ4. Traffic sources are anonymised numeric codes, so this dataset is not used for channel comparison.

c) Criteo Attribution Modeling for Bidding (Diemert et al., 2017): 30 days of live display advertising traffic with 16.5 million impressions, about 45,000 conversions and 700 campaigns, including click flags, click position and Criteo's own last-click attribution label. All impressions belong to a single channel (Criteo display ads), so it is used as a secondary source for RQ3: how credit is distributed across repeated impressions within a journey, the value of clicked versus viewed impressions, and the gap between conversions Criteo's ads touched and conversions Criteo was credited with under last-click.

d) Facebook Ad Campaign dataset (Kaggle, anonymised company): 1,143 real Facebook ads across three campaigns, with spend, impressions, clicks, total and approved conversions, and audience age, gender and interest. Used to validate the plug-and-play module and the AI assistant on real ad-platform data (RQ5).

Business inputs supported by the plug-and-play module:
a) Ad performance CSV exported from Google Ads or Meta Ads Manager (campaign, spend, impressions, clicks, conversions, conversion value). Produces CTR, CPC, CPA, conversion rate and ROAS per campaign, flags wasted spend and identifies campaigns worth scaling.
b) Customer journey CSV (user ID, timestamp, channel, converted), exportable from GA4, a CRM or UTM logs. Produces journey paths and a side-by-side comparison of all seven attribution models.
Two formats are needed because ad-platform exports contain only campaign-level totals; true multi-touch attribution requires journey-level data.

Datasets considered and not selected: the GA4 obfuscated e-commerce sample (only three months, heavier obfuscation; kept as a backup for RQ1 and RQ3); Kaggle synthetic multi-touch attribution datasets (generated data, unsuitable for findings; used only as format test files); and the MAC benchmark from Taobao (single-platform, designed for conversion-rate prediction rather than channel attribution).

8. Methodology
Step 1. Data preparation: remove duplicates, fix formats, map channels, and document every cleaning decision.
Step 2. Exploratory analysis: conversion rates by channel, visitor type, month and weekday.
Step 3. Journey reconstruction (GA360): order each visitor's sessions by visit start time, record the channel of each session, and cut the path at the first transaction. Non-converting paths are kept for comparison.
Step 4. Attribution: apply the five rule-based models and two data-driven models (Markov chain removal effect and Shapley value). Compare each channel's share of credit and its rank across models.
Step 5. Validation of attribution: (i) test the implementation on synthetic journeys with a known correct answer; (ii) check that credits sum to total conversions under every model; (iii) report 95 percent bootstrap confidence intervals for each channel's credit share; (iv) check the Markov model's predictive fit on held-out journeys; (v) for Criteo, compare our last-touch results with the dataset's own attribution label.
Step 6. Conversion analysis: Mann-Whitney U and chi-square tests for group differences; logistic regression, random forest and gradient boosting classifiers evaluated by ROC-AUC on a stratified held-out test set, with permutation feature importance. Models are run with and without the PageValues feature, because it is partly derived from the outcome.
Step 7. Productisation: convert the analysis into reusable modules that run on uploaded CSV files.
Step 8. Evaluation of the tool: (i) correctness, by comparing the tool's outputs on the real Facebook dataset and on synthetic Google Ads files against manually computed values; (ii) usability, through a System Usability Scale (SUS) questionnaire with 8 to 10 users such as marketing students or small business owners; (iii) assistant accuracy, by asking a fixed set of about 30 questions and scoring each answer as correct, partially correct or unsupported against the computed facts.
Step 9. Interpretation and paper: separate evidence from interpretation, state limitations and present findings as associations rather than causal claims.

9. Tools and Technology (zero cost)
Language and analysis: Python, Pandas, NumPy, SciPy, scikit-learn.
Visualisation and web app: Plotly and Streamlit.
Hosting: Streamlit Community Cloud (free) with source code on GitHub.
AI assistant: free-tier large language model APIs (Google Gemini or Groq), with a built-in offline mode that works without any API key or internet connection.
Data access: Google BigQuery sandbox (free tier) for the Google Analytics sample; direct download for UCI, Criteo and the Kaggle Facebook dataset.

10. Work Completed So Far
A working prototype of ChannelTrace has been built with an overview page, a Research Findings dashboard and the AI assistant. Preliminary results on the UCI dataset (12,205 sessions after removing 125 duplicates):
a) Overall conversion rate is 15.6 percent.
b) New visitors convert at 24.9 percent compared with 14.1 percent for returning visitors, although returning visitors make up 85 percent of sessions (chi-square p < 0.001).
c) Conversion peaks in November at 25.5 percent and is lowest in February at 1.7 percent.
d) Converting sessions view a median of 29 product pages against 16 for non-converting sessions and spend a median of 18.5 minutes on product pages against 8.8 minutes; all ten behavioural measures differ significantly (Mann-Whitney U, p < 0.05).
e) A random forest predicts conversion with ROC-AUC 0.93 including PageValues and 0.78 without it. Without PageValues, the strongest signals are time on product pages, exit rate and the November season.
The AI assistant currently answers questions about these findings and runs in offline mode when no API key is configured. The suitability of each dataset has been checked against its official documentation, which led to the dataset roles described in Section 7.

13. Scope and Limitations
a) The Google Analytics sample is obfuscated, has some fields removed and comes from a single online store; journeys are limited to what one website can observe (for example, ad views that did not lead to a visit are not recorded).
b) Criteo covers one channel (display advertising) and UCI traffic sources are anonymised codes; neither is used for cross-channel comparison.
c) Attribution assigns credit; it does not prove that a channel caused a purchase. There is no ground-truth attribution in observational data, so the study compares models and tests their stability rather than claiming one model is correct. All findings are presented as associations.
d) The datasets come from different contexts and time periods, so findings are compared across studies, not pooled.
e) The AI assistant can make mistakes. It is restricted to the computed facts, every recommendation is labelled as a suggestion, and its accuracy is measured and reported.
f) Business data uploaded to the tool is processed in the session only and is not stored.
