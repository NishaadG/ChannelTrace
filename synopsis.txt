CHANNELTRACE
Multi-Channel Digital Marketing Attribution and Conversion Analysis
Synopsis

1. Abstract
Customers rarely buy after seeing a single ad. A typical buyer may see a social media post, later search on Google, open an email and only then make a purchase. This project studies such multi-channel customer journeys using public digital marketing and e-commerce data, compares how different attribution models divide the credit for a conversion among channels, and identifies which visitor and session behaviours are associated with a purchase. The research methods are then packaged into ChannelTrace, a free web application with three parts: a dashboard presenting our research findings, a plug-and-play module where a business uploads its own Google Ads, Meta (Facebook) Ads or customer-journey CSV and receives an insight dashboard, and an AI assistant that explains the results in plain language and suggests actions. The entire project is built with free and open-source tools at zero cost.

2. Problem Statement
Businesses can see that a sale happened but usually cannot tell which marketing interactions led to it. Each ad platform reports its own view, typically last-click, so several platforms may claim credit for the same sale. We identify three gaps. First, a measurement gap: the credit given to a channel changes considerably depending on the attribution rule used, and most small teams never compare rules. Second, a tooling gap: attribution tools are either tied to one platform or require paid subscriptions. Third, an interpretation gap: even when numbers are available, small business owners often do not know what action to take from them.

3. Aim
To study how multiple digital marketing touchpoints relate to customer conversion, to show how different attribution models change the credit assigned to each channel, and to deliver these methods as a free, easy-to-use tool that businesses can apply to their own data.

4. Objectives
a) Collect, clean and document three complementary public datasets.
b) Reconstruct customer journeys and identify common paths to purchase.
c) Compare six attribution models: first touch, last touch, linear, time decay, position based and a data-driven Markov chain model.
d) Identify session and visitor behaviours associated with conversion using statistical tests and machine learning.
e) Build an interactive dashboard that presents the research findings.
f) Build a plug-and-play module that accepts business CSV exports from Google Ads, Meta Ads or journey logs and generates an insight dashboard automatically.
g) Build an AI assistant that answers questions and suggests actions, grounded only in the computed results.
h) Write a research paper covering methodology, results, limitations and practical implications.

5. Research Questions
RQ1. What are the common digital touchpoint journeys before a customer converts?
RQ2. How do converting and non-converting journeys or sessions differ?
RQ3. Does the estimated importance of a channel change when a different attribution model is used?
RQ4. Which customer or session characteristics are associated with a higher likelihood of conversion?
RQ5. Can these methods be packaged so that a small business obtains useful, plain-language insights from its own marketing data?

6. Proposed Solution
The project has three connected components.
Research study: journey, attribution and conversion analysis on public datasets, reported in a research paper.
ChannelTrace dashboard: a web application with a Research Findings section and a Your Data section. In Your Data, a business uploads a CSV; the tool detects the file type and column names, cleans the data and generates the dashboard with no login, API setup or cost.
AI marketing assistant: a chatbot that reads the dashboard's computed metrics (never the raw uploaded data) and answers questions such as "Which campaign should I cut?" or "When should I run my biggest campaign?" in plain language.

This differs from a conventional reporting dashboard in three ways: it is driven by research questions, it works on any business's own data rather than one fixed dataset, and it compares several attribution models instead of showing a single number.

7. Data
Research datasets:
a) UCI Online Shoppers Purchasing Intention (Sakar and Kastro, 2018): 12,330 website sessions with 17 features and a purchase outcome. Used for RQ2 and RQ4.
b) Criteo Attribution Modeling for Bidding dataset (Diemert et al., 2017): 30 days of anonymised display advertising traffic with about 16.5 million impressions, about 45,000 conversions and 700 campaigns. Used for RQ3.
c) Google Merchandise Store GA4 sample on BigQuery (Google): three months of obfuscated event-level e-commerce data with traffic sources. Used for RQ1.
The datasets come from different populations and will not be merged; each answers a different research question.

Business inputs supported by the plug-and-play module:
a) Ad performance CSV exported from Google Ads or Meta Ads Manager (campaign, spend, impressions, clicks, conversions, conversion value). Produces CTR, CPC, CPA, conversion rate and ROAS per campaign, flags wasted spend and identifies campaigns worth scaling.
b) Customer journey CSV (user ID, timestamp, channel, converted), exportable from GA4, a CRM or UTM logs. Produces journey paths and a side-by-side comparison of all six attribution models.
Two formats are needed because ad platform exports contain only campaign totals; true multi-touch attribution requires journey-level data.

8. Methodology
Step 1. Data preparation: remove duplicates, fix formats, document every cleaning decision.
Step 2. Exploratory analysis: conversion rates by visitor type, month, traffic source and weekday.
Step 3. Journey reconstruction: order each user's touchpoints by time and cut the path at the first conversion.
Step 4. Attribution: apply the five rule-based models and the Markov chain removal-effect model; compare the share of credit and the rank of each channel across models, with sanity checks that credits sum to total conversions.
Step 5. Conversion analysis: Mann-Whitney U and chi-square tests for group differences; logistic regression and random forest classifiers evaluated by ROC-AUC on a held-out test set, with permutation feature importance. Models are run both with and without the PageValues feature, because it is partly derived from the outcome.
Step 6. Productisation: convert the analysis into reusable modules that run on uploaded CSV files.
Step 7. AI assistant: generate a fact sheet of computed results and pass only that to a large language model with instructions to answer strictly from those facts.
Step 8. Interpretation and paper: separate evidence from interpretation, state limitations and present findings as associations rather than causal claims.

9. Tools and Technology (zero cost)
Language and analysis: Python, Pandas, NumPy, SciPy, scikit-learn.
Visualisation and web app: Plotly and Streamlit.
Hosting: Streamlit Community Cloud (free) with source code on GitHub.
AI assistant: free-tier large language model APIs (Google Gemini or Groq), with a built-in offline mode that works without any API key or internet connection.
Data access: Google BigQuery sandbox (free tier) for the GA4 sample; direct download for UCI and Criteo.

10. Work Completed So Far
A working prototype of ChannelTrace has been built with an overview page, a Research Findings dashboard and the AI assistant. Preliminary results on the UCI dataset (12,205 sessions after removing 125 duplicates):
a) Overall conversion rate is 15.6 percent.
b) New visitors convert at 24.9 percent compared with 14.1 percent for returning visitors, although returning visitors make up 85 percent of sessions (chi-square p < 0.001).
c) Conversion peaks in November at 25.5 percent and is lowest in February at 1.7 percent.
d) Converting sessions view a median of 29 product pages against 16 for non-converting sessions and spend a median of 18.5 minutes on product pages against 8.8 minutes; all ten behavioural measures differ significantly (Mann-Whitney U, p < 0.05).
e) A random forest predicts conversion with ROC-AUC 0.93 including PageValues and 0.78 without it. Without PageValues, the strongest signals are time on product pages, exit rate and the November season.
The AI assistant currently answers questions about these findings and runs in offline mode when no API key is configured.

13. Scope and Limitations
a) Public datasets do not contain every marketing channel; Criteo covers display advertising only and UCI traffic sources are anonymised codes.
b) The GA4 sample is obfuscated and covers only three months.
c) Attribution assigns credit; it does not prove that a channel caused a purchase. All findings are presented as associations.
d) The AI assistant can make mistakes. It is restricted to the computed facts and every recommendation is labelled as a suggestion.
e) Business data uploaded to the tool is processed in the session only and is not stored.
